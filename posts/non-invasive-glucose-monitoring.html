Non-Invasive Glucose Monitoring: Closer Than We Think?

By Beyond Diabetes · Digital Infrastructure Chair

The promise of a glucose monitor that requires no needle, no sensor, and no calibration has driven billions of research dollars over the past two decades. It has also produced a graveyard of failed companies. But in 2026, renewed interest — and more rigorous science — is prompting a second look.

Why it's hard

Glucose is present in interstitial fluid, blood, saliva, and sweat — but at concentrations that are difficult to isolate from background biological noise using non-invasive sensing. Spectroscopic approaches must distinguish glucose's molecular signature from thousands of other compounds, all through skin that varies in thickness, hydration, and pigmentation across individuals and even within the same person across days.

Apple has reportedly invested heavily in optical glucose monitoring research for the Apple Watch. Leaks and SEC filings have suggested the technology may be approaching consumer readiness, though regulatory clearance remains a significant hurdle. Independent verification of their claimed accuracy is still unavailable.

Optical approaches

Near-infrared (NIR) and mid-infrared (MIR) spectroscopy use light absorption patterns to infer glucose concentration. Samsung, Rockley Photonics, and several startups are pursuing this path. Early clinical data is promising in controlled conditions but accuracy under real-world variation remains problematic. Calibration drift is a significant unsolved challenge.

Acoustic & other modalities

Photoacoustic spectroscopy — which uses pulsed light to generate ultrasonic waves that can be measured — has shown some of the best non-invasive accuracy data in recent literature. Companies like GlucoLight and academic groups at MIT and Cambridge have published results suggesting MARD values in the 10-15% range, which is still above current CGM benchmarks but represents significant progress.

Realistic timeline

Most experts in the field believe a regulatory-cleared, clinically accurate non-invasive glucose monitor is 3-7 years away at minimum. The FDA's clearance bar for CGMs is high, and non-invasive devices face even greater scrutiny given their history of overpromising. The technology is advancing, but tempering expectations remains appropriate.
